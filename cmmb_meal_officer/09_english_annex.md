# English annex

*Module 9 — the job description is written in English and lists fluency in French, Haitian Creole
and English as a must. Expect part of the conversation to switch to English, if only to check.
This annex gives you the pitch, the vocabulary, and the answers most likely to be asked in
English. If a question comes in English, **answer in English** until they switch back.*

---

## 1. The ninety-second pitch, in English

> I am an applied economist by training, a graduate of CTPEA, and I have spent more than five
> years working at the intersection of monitoring and evaluation and data management in Haiti.
>
> The core of my experience, and the part that maps most directly onto this position, is the
> three years I spent as Monitoring and Evaluation Officer on an HIV program at Caris Foundation
> International. I ran the routine reporting cycle for multi-site clinics and reported MER
> indicators to USAID through DATIM, PEPFAR's DHIS2-based platform. That is where I established
> and supervised the data quality assurance protocols behind those figures — verification that
> traces a reported number back to the source register, and corrective actions followed through
> to closure rather than merely logged. I also supervised field data collection staff across the
> sites.
>
> Around that, I built two things. First, digital data collection: at Anseye Pou Ayiti and
> HANWASH I coded XLSForm questionnaires for ODK and KoboToolbox with skip logic and validation
> constraints, deployed geospatial surveys on mWater, administered the database, and trained
> field teams on those tools. Second, data engineering: I am a backend developer, I administer
> relational databases, and I deploy and maintain the systems themselves, not only the indicators
> they produce.
>
> That is why your posting caught my attention specifically. It describes two jobs. One is
> classic M&E — reporting cycles, feedback to health facilities, data quality assessment,
> surveys, training. The other is health information system operations — infrastructure, version
> deployment across sites, incident documentation, keeping the database running. Most M&E
> candidates can only take on the first half. I can take on both, and I am based in
> Port-au-Prince and available for site visits.

## 3. Six answers to rehearse in English

### "What do you know about CMMB?"

> CMMB was founded in 1912 by Dr. Paluel Flagg, a New York anesthesiologist — and the detail that
> struck me is that he began his work in Haiti, caring for people with Hansen's disease. Haiti
> isn't a later expansion for CMMB; it is where the organization started, and it is now your
> largest country office.
>
> On HIV, you joined the AIDSRelief consortium in 2004, then led the continuation as SIDALE from
> 2011 with CDC funding, and then ALESIDA across thirteen partner hospitals. You also run CHAMPS
> and the Bishop Joseph Sullivan hospital in Côtes-de-Fer since 2017, Klinik Sen Damien in the
> north, and water, sanitation and emergency response work.
>
> And one point that concerns me directly: CMMB was a partner in the Projet Santé consortium led
> by Caris Foundation, covering the north. Caris Foundation is where I worked for three years as
> M&E Officer — not on Projet Santé itself, I was on the Impact Youth project, on HIV. But it was
> the same organization, the same donor, and the same reporting chain to USAID and PEPFAR.

### "Walk me through the cascade of care."

> The cascade describes the path of a person living with HIV in narrowing steps: everyone living
> with HIV, then those who know their status, then those on antiretroviral treatment, then those
> who are virally suppressed. UNAIDS set the 95-95-95 target for 2025, replacing the 90-90-90
> target set for 2020.
>
> The thing to be careful about is that the three rates are conditional — each one is calculated
> on the previous step — so they multiply. Reaching 95-95-95 means roughly 86 percent of all
> people living with HIV are virally suppressed, not 95 percent.
>
> And one point of vocabulary, because it gets shortened a lot: the third 95 is about viral
> **suppression**, under 1,000 copies per millilitre, not about being **undetectable**, which is
> a lower threshold around 50 and belongs to the U=U message. The 1,000 threshold was chosen
> because it stays measurable on the platforms available in resource-limited settings, including
> dried blood spot samples.
>
> It's also worth saying that 95-95-95 is only the **first** of UNAIDS's three HIV services
> targets for 2025 — what people often call the "six 95s". And the first one is stated as
> achieved *within all sub-populations and age groups*, which is an equity requirement: a national
> average of 95% hiding 60% among adolescents does not meet the target. That's what makes
> disaggregation part of the definition rather than good practice. The second target carries three
> more 95s and is essentially your portfolio — sexual and reproductive health needs met for women
> of reproductive age, viral suppression in pregnant and breastfeeding women before delivery, and
> HIV-exposed infants tested with **results returned to the parents** by two months. The third is
> combination prevention for people at risk.
>
> There's also an asymmetry worth naming. The denominator of the first 95 — the total number of
> people living with HIV — isn't counted, it's modelled from population surveys. The other two
> come from program registers. So a country's first 95 can move without anything changing in the
> field, simply because the estimate was revised.

### "What did you do with MER indicators?"

> I was on the data producer side. I consolidated site-level data, applied the MER definitions
> for the version of the guide in force, built the required age and sex disaggregations, ran
> consistency checks, had the figures validated internally, and submitted them in DATIM ahead of
> the PEPFAR reporting deadline, then handled the feedback.
>
> Let me be precise on one point, because the distinction matters: I was not a DHIS2 platform
> administrator. Metadata configuration and system-wide user management were not mine. What I do
> administer, day to day and in a separate role, is relational databases — MySQL and PostgreSQL —
> and that is where the systems half of your posting connects to my background.

### "A site reports 98 percent viral suppression. What's your reaction?"

> I'd ask for the coverage before commenting on the rate. The TX_PVLS denominator is patients on
> treatment with a documented viral load result in the last twelve months, not everyone on
> treatment. A site that only tests its best-followed patients will show an excellent rate on a
> very small denominator. So I divide that denominator by TX_CURR to get viral load coverage, and
> I never present a suppression rate without stating its coverage in the same sentence.
> Ninety-eight percent on thirty percent coverage isn't a good result — it's missing information.

### "Why should we hire you rather than a pure M&E candidate?"

> Because your posting asks for two things and I do both. Take one line from it: documenting
> technical incidents that leave a facility without its information system, because that creates
> a backlog and prevents clinical staff from reporting. Most people treat that as an
> administrative task. I treat it as a data quality event.
>
> When a site loses its system for three weeks, care continues but recording falls back to paper.
> When the system returns, someone enters three weeks of backlog in a few days, often without
> having seen the patients. That retrospective bulk entry is structurally less reliable than
> real-time entry — dates cluster on entry days, optional fields get skipped, some encounters are
> never captured at all. So that site's indicator for that month should be published with a
> caveat, and the incident log belongs next to the indicators rather than in a separate IT file.
>
> A purely M&E profile doesn't make that link because they don't see the outage. A purely IT
> profile doesn't make it because they don't report the indicator.

### "How would a program respond to reduced funding?"

> The instinct is to cut proportionally everywhere, and that's the worst option. The cascade
> tells you where cutting costs least. Low-yield testing activities can be narrowed and better
> targeted, because their positivity is measurable. Treatment continuity cannot be cut, because
> an interruption produces resistance, re-hospitalization and onward transmission — a future cost
> larger than the present saving.
>
> The role of M&E in that situation isn't to document the decline. It's to supply the information
> that makes the trade-off possible: which site, which modality, which yield. When resources
> shrink, data quality stops being a luxury and becomes the decision tool.

## 4. If your English falters

Two things will happen under pressure: you will reach for a French word, and you will speed up.
Both are manageable, and neither is disqualifying for a role where French and Creole are the
working languages of the field.

If a term escapes you, do not stop and apologize. Say the concept in plain words and move on —
*the number we divide by* is a perfectly good stand-in for *denominator*, and any M&E person
will supply the word for you. If you genuinely need the French term, take it: *the — in French we
say* **facteur de vérification** *— the ratio of the recounted figure to the reported one*. In a
trilingual office in Port-au-Prince, that reads as bilingual competence, not as a gap.

And slow down deliberately. A candidate who speaks slowly in a second language sounds
considered; a candidate who rushes sounds unsure. Short sentences, one idea each. That is also,
incidentally, better English.
