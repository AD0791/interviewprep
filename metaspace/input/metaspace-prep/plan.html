01 The read

They sourced you from a Kotlin repository and their outreach email asks for Solidity. You've confirmed the actual role is backend with some frontend. Those two facts together tell you what the hour will be: a backend interview with a web3 literacy check attached, run by someone who probably wrote the contracts themselves.

So the goal is not to pass as a blockchain engineer. It's to be obviously strong on backend and data, and precisely credible on chain mechanics — fluent in the vocabulary, honest about the edges. A senior engineer will trust a candidate who says "I haven't shipped a production contract, but here's the integration layer I built" far more than one who bluffs and folds on the second follow-up.

The leverage is concentrated. Two ideas — a reorg-safe indexer and a server-signed mint voucher — cover most of what a game backend actually does on-chain. Everything else on this page is support for those two.

02 If you only get three hours

The honest 80/20. Do these in this order and you are already in the top half of their candidate pool.

The irreducible core

  1. Rehearse the 60-second positioning answer out loud, five times. It is asked in every interview and it sets your level for the whole hour. It's written for you in notes/positioning.md.
  2. Be able to draw the indexer on a whiteboard. Checkpoint, confirmations, block-hash comparison, rollback, idempotent upsert. Narrate why contract.on("Transfer") is the wrong answer.
  3. Explain the signed mint voucher and name its four replay defences — chainId, verifyingContract, nonce, deadline.
  4. Narrate a transaction's full lifecycle, from wallet signature to your database updating. Smoothness here reads as experience.
  5. Say the boundary out loud: the server is authoritative, the chain is a settlement layer, not a rules engine.

06 Two things worth being careful about

Don't run interview code on your main machine

Metaspace checks out on the public record — licensed by RAK DAO in Ras Al Khaimah, a live Polygon mobile game, and the .org domain in the email redirects to their real site. This doesn't look like an impersonation.

But there's an active, well-documented campaign that targets developers with malicious "technical interview" repositories, aimed specifically at people with crypto on their machines — and your GitHub makes you exactly the profile it looks for. So treat it as ordinary hygiene rather than suspicion: if they send a take-home, read it before running it, run it in a container or a throwaway VM, and never connect a wallet holding real funds to anything that arrives during a hiring process. Nobody legitimate will mind.

The one small oddity: the scheduling link is a personal Calendly handle rather than a company-branded one. Common at small studios, and asking for names and roles at the start of the call settles it either way.
