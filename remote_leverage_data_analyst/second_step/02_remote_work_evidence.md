# Remote-work evidence sheet

*The highest-stakes module in this folder. They already asked about remote experience in the group session, which means it is on their scorecard. Fill in the blanks with measured numbers before the call — not estimates.*

---

## 1. The problem this module solves

Almost every candidate answers the remote-work question the same way: *"Yes, I have remote experience, I have good internet, I'm very disciplined."*

None of that is information. The recruiter has heard it forty times this week, including from candidates who then missed calls because the power went out. Because the sentence carries no data, it cannot move their assessment, and the assessment defaults to whatever they already assumed about your location.

You have an unusually strong hand here and you are currently playing it as though it were a weak one. Look at what your resume actually says: **Tekkod, remote, March 2021 to May 2024. HANWASH, remote via Upwork, October 2023 to January 2026. Anseye Pou Ayiti, remote, 2026. Tekkod again, remote, February 2026 to present.** That is roughly five years of continuous remote delivery for distributed teams, including a multi-year engagement on a global freelance platform where the client could have terminated at any moment and did not.

Most candidates in that group call have a year of pandemic remote work at a local employer. You have half a decade of cross-border contract delivery. The task is not to acquire evidence. It is to say it in numbers instead of adjectives.

---

## 2. The Haiti question, handled head-on

Be clear-eyed about this. Remote Leverage advertises sourcing from Latin America and the Philippines. A US client seeing "Haiti" on a profile may bring assumptions about electricity, internet and stability, and those assumptions will not usually be voiced — they will just quietly downgrade you.

Two facts work in your favour and you should deploy both early.

**Your timezone is an advantage, not a neutral.** Haiti runs on Eastern time. Their Philippine talent pool is eleven to twelve hours offset from a US client, working nights to overlap. You are on the client's clock natively, full overlap, no night shift, no handover lag. Say this out loud in the call. It is the single most persuasive geographic fact you own and it costs the client nothing.

**Your track record already answers the infrastructure question.** You did not merely work remotely — you delivered on Upwork for a client from October 2023 to January 2026, twenty-seven months, on a platform that logs everything and where an unreliable contractor is replaced within weeks. That is not a claim, it is a public record with a rating attached.

The move is to **volunteer the infrastructure numbers before you are asked**, folded into the answer to a general remote-work question. Pre-empting a doubt costs one sentence. Dismantling one after it has formed costs the interview.

## 4. The answers, written to be spoken

### "Do you have experience working remotely?"

This is the question they already asked in the group session, so expect it again in more depth. The structure that works: duration first, then a named engagement, then the infrastructure numbers unprompted, then the timezone advantage.

> "I've worked remotely for about five years now, and effectively all of my work has been remote — I'm currently a software engineer at Tekkod, which is a fully distributed team, and before that I spent a bit over two years as a consultant for HANWASH through Upwork, working with a US-based team the whole time. So this isn't a transition for me, it's just how I work.
>
> On the practical side, since I know it comes up: I'm on fibre at [X] down and [X] up, I keep a mobile hotspot as a documented fallback, and I have battery backup that holds my full workstation for [X] hours, so a power cut doesn't take me off a call. And one thing that's actually useful for a US client — I'm in Haiti, which is Eastern time. Full overlap with a US working day, no night shift, no waiting until tomorrow for an answer."

Three things that paragraph does deliberately. It gives a duration and two named engagements rather than an adjective. It puts the infrastructure numbers on the table before anyone can wonder about them. And it converts the geography from a possible liability into a stated advantage in the same breath — which is much stronger than defending it later.

### "How do you handle communication across time zones?"

The trap is answering with tools. Everybody says Slack.

> "The thing I've learned is that the tools matter much less than the habit of writing down what you did and what you're blocked on before anyone asks. On the Tekkod work, most of the team wasn't in the same place at the same time, so I got used to leaving a short written update — what moved, what's stuck, what I need from someone — so that when they came online they could act on it immediately rather than having to schedule a call with me to find out. I'd rather over-communicate. In my experience the thing that breaks remote work isn't a missed meeting, it's someone quietly stuck for two days."

### "How do we know you'll be reliable / how do you stay disciplined?"

Do not answer with a personality claim. Answer with a structure and a record.

> "The honest answer is that I don't rely on discipline, I rely on a fixed structure — I keep the same working hours every day, I'm online at the start of the client's day, and I keep a visible list of what's in progress. And I'd point at the record rather than at myself: I was on that Upwork engagement for over two years, which on a platform like that doesn't happen unless the client is getting what they need every week. If you'd like, my contacts at Tekkod and HANWASH are both happy to speak to that."

Offering the referees inside the answer is what makes it land. It converts a claim into something checkable, and recruiters relax visibly when a candidate volunteers verification.

### "What happens if your internet or power goes down mid-task?"

They may not ask it this directly. Answer it anyway inside question one.

> "I've got a mobile hotspot that I can be back online through in under a minute, and battery backup for [X] hours on the workstation. The other half of that is communication — if something did take me offline for longer, the client would hear it from me immediately with an ETA, not find out when I missed a deadline. I've never had an outage cost a client a deliverable."

Only say the last sentence if it is true. If it is not, say instead: *"I've had outages, and what mattered was that the client knew within minutes and never had to guess."* That version is nearly as strong and it is honest.

### "What hours can you commit to?"

Be specific and generous, but do not promise anything you will resent in month three. A committed window you actually hold beats a wide window you drift out of.

> "I can commit to [8:00 to 17:00] Eastern as a core block, five days a week, which should be full overlap with most US clients. I'm flexible on the edges of that for a standing meeting if a team needs it — I just prefer that it's scheduled rather than ad hoc, because that's what lets me protect deep work time."

## 5. The three stories to have ready

Behavioural questions in a remote-placement screen almost always reduce to these three. Prepare one specific story each — a real project, a real outcome, ninety seconds spoken.

**A time you delivered without supervision.** The Upwork engagement with HANWASH is the natural candidate: you deployed mWater surveys, resolved field collection issues, and built the Power BI and mWater dashboards, largely on your own judgement, for a client who was not in the room.

**A time you handled a problem or a mistake.** The strongest technical version you own is the Tekkod query optimisation — thirty-six seconds down to under half a second. It works as a "how do you approach a hard problem" story and needs no translation into commercial language. Have a second, humbler one where something actually went wrong and you caught it; the DQA protocol work at Caris is fertile ground.

**A time you worked with someone difficult or with unclear requirements.** Field-team training and multi-site data flows at Caris and Anseye Pou Ayiti give you real material. The point of the story is not the conflict, it is what you did to make the requirement concrete.
