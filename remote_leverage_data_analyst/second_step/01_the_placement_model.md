# The placement model, and what it means for you

*Read this once, carefully. Almost every strange or uncomfortable question in stage two makes sense once you understand where the money comes from.*

---

## 1. Start with the money, because everything follows from it

Most people meet a staffing agency and assume it works like an employer with extra steps. It does not, and the difference determines what you should say.

In a **traditional staffing arrangement**, the agency employs you, bills the client a marked-up hourly rate, and keeps the spread. You are paid by the agency. The agency earns for as long as you stay, which means the agency's interests and yours are broadly aligned: they want you placed, retained, and eventually re-rated upward, because their revenue is a percentage of yours in perpetuity.

Remote Leverage does not work that way. They charge the client a **one-time flat fee** for the introduction, and then step out. The client pays you directly. A partner company handles payroll and compliance.

Sit with the consequence for a second, because it is the single most useful thing in this folder. **Their revenue is a function of placement volume, not of your salary or your tenure.** They earn the same fee whether you are placed at $1,000 a month or $3,000 a month, and they earn nothing extra for your second year.

---

## 2. What that changes in the conversation

Three practical consequences.

**They are not motivated to negotiate your rate upward.** In a margin-based model, a recruiter arguing for a higher rate is arguing for their own commission. Here they are not. The rate is set between the client's budget and your acceptance, and the agency's only interest is that the number is one both sides will sign. That means **nobody in the room is advocating for your price except you.** This is not villainy; it is arithmetic. But it means you walk in with your floor already decided, because you will not be talked upward by anyone else. [Module 04](04_rate_and_engagement_terms.md) is that number.

**Speed matters enormously to them.** Volume model, one-time fee — the constraint on their business is throughput. A candidate who responds within an hour, has documents ready, and books the next call immediately is worth more to them than a slightly stronger candidate who takes three days to reply. Responsiveness during the process is itself a screening signal, and it is one of the cheapest advantages available to you. Reply fast, always.

**Failed placements are expensive to them.** If there is a guarantee period — and there almost always is — a candidate who quits or is dismissed inside it means the agency does the search again for one fee. That is why stage two is dominated by reliability questions rather than skill questions. Every "do you have backup internet" and "what hours can you commit" is really "will this person still be working in ninety days."

Understanding this reframes how you answer. You are not proving you are impressive. You are **removing reasons to worry.**

---

## 3. The candidate-supply constraint, and why English keeps coming up

Because the VA is paid directly by the client, the agency's growth is not limited by how much capital it has to float payroll. It is limited by how many placeable candidates it can find. And "placeable" for a US client buying offshore support means, above all, English that does not create friction.

That is why English is not one attribute among many in their database — it is the product specification. Expect the live call to be the assessment, expect a recorded component, and expect the recruiter to be listening to *how* you answer at least as much as *what* you answer. Length, filler, and hesitation cost you more here than a slightly imperfect technical claim would.

You are genuinely fluent, and your resume says so. The risk is not fluency, it is being unrehearsed — spoken English under evaluation pressure is a different skill from spoken English in a working meeting. Twenty minutes a day out loud between now and the call closes that gap almost entirely.

## 4. Where Haiti sits in this, and why you should raise it first

Remote Leverage's public positioning is that it recruits from **Latin America and the Philippines**. You are in Haiti. Geographically Haiti is in the Caribbean and in the LatAm-and-Caribbean grouping that most US firms use, and your timezone is Eastern — which is better than most of their Philippine talent pool for a US client. But a recruiter scanning a list may not have Haiti on it, and a US client reading "Haiti" may bring assumptions about infrastructure and stability.

There are two ways to handle this. The passive way is to wait and see whether it comes up. The active way is to put your infrastructure evidence on the table early, unprompted, in the answer to a related question — before anyone has time to form a doubt you then have to argue against.

Take the active way. It is almost always easier to prevent a concern than to dismantle one. The mechanics are in [module 02](02_remote_work_evidence.md); the principle is here: **volunteer the numbers before you are asked for them.**

There is also a question you need answered, and it is better asked plainly than guessed at: *do you currently place candidates based in Haiti, and are there any payment or compliance constraints there?* If the answer is no, you have lost one call instead of three weeks. If the answer is yes, you have just eliminated the largest unknown in the process. Either outcome is worth the ten seconds.

## 5. What "Hiring Manager/Client" means for stage three

In the in-house reading of this process, stage three would have been Remote Leverage's own manager assessing you against their own needs. In the placement reading, stage three is an **interview with a company that is not Remote Leverage**, arranged by Remote Leverage, for a specific open role.

Three things follow.

The interview is **requisition-specific**, so preparation is impossible until you know the requisition. Getting that information out of stage two is a stated objective, not a nice-to-have.

Your interviewer is **the person who will actually pay you**, which means the rate conversation may reopen at stage three even if it seemed settled at stage two. Know your floor going in and hold the same number in both conversations. Inconsistency here is the most common way candidates lose credibility late in a process.

And the client is buying a **specific function**, not a versatile person. Whatever breadth you present to the recruiter, you present focus to the client. That distinction is the entire subject of [module 03](03_dual_track_positioning.md).

---

## 6. Due diligence, kept in proportion

The public signal is positive — Glassdoor shows roughly 4.9 out of 5 across about 299 reviews. An industry comparison noted the absence of verified client feedback on Clutch or Trustpilot, which is worth knowing but is not by itself alarming for a company of this size and model.

The ordinary precautions apply and none of them require you to be suspicious. Never pay a fee to be placed — a legitimate agency is paid by the client, and any request for money from the candidate ends the conversation. Never share banking credentials, government ID numbers or documents before there is a signed agreement and a named entity to send them to. Expect a written contract before you start work, and read the clauses on the guarantee period, notice, and any exclusivity or non-circumvention terms, since the last of those can restrict you from working with the client directly later.

Asking about these calmly is not rude. A company whose entire product is cross-border remote work will have crisp answers, and an inability to answer clearly is itself information.
