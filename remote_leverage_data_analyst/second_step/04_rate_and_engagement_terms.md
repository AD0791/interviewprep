# Rate, contract, and engagement terms

*Decide your number before the call, while you are calm. You will not decide it well while someone is waiting for you to answer.*

---

## 1. Why the number from the job posting no longer applies

`first_step/` worked from the figure in the posting: **$1,920–$2,080 per month**, and treated it as near-fixed, which was correct advice for an in-house hire against a published band.

That figure now tells you much less than it did. It was the band Remote Leverage set for a role *they* were filling. In the placement model the rate is set by **the client's budget for a specific requisition**, and it varies enormously — a US small business hiring general support and a US software company hiring a backend developer are not drawing from the same pool of money, even through the same agency.

So treat the posted band as one useful data point rather than as the answer. It tells you roughly where this agency's requisitions sit and it is probably a reasonable centre of gravity. But a technical requisition can sit well above it, and a general-VA requisition sits well below, and the difference between those two outcomes is decided in stage two by how you talk about level.

---

## 2. Nobody in the room is advocating for your price

Worth restating from [module 01](01_the_placement_model.md), because it is the fact that most changes behaviour.

On a one-time flat-fee model, the agency earns the same fee whether you are placed at $1,000 or $3,000 a month. In a traditional margin-based staffing arrangement the recruiter has a financial stake in your rate; here they do not. Their interest is that the number is one both sides sign quickly.

This is not adversarial and you should not treat the recruiter as an opponent — they are genuinely trying to place you, and a placement that makes you happy is a placement that survives the guarantee period. But it does mean **you are the only person in the process who will argue for your price**. Prepare accordingly.

---

## 3. Decide these three numbers now

Write them down before the call. One number each, not ranges you will improvise around.

| | Number | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Floor** | | Below this you decline. The number where you would rather keep your current work. |
| **Target** | | What you are aiming at. Say this one out loud if forced to give a figure. |
| **Stated range** | | If you must give a range, it runs from your target upward. Never from your floor. |

The last row is the one people get wrong. If you say "$1,500 to $2,000" you will be offered $1,500 — every time, without exception. A range is heard as its bottom. So the bottom of any range you state must already be a number you are pleased with.

Some inputs to help you set the floor honestly. Your current Tekkod engagement establishes what you are giving up. The posted band of $1,920–2,080 tells you where this agency's requisitions cluster. Your profile is technical, senior, five-plus years, Eastern time, fluent English — which places you at the top of an offshore support pool, not the middle of it. And a monthly figure with no benefits, no paid leave and self-managed taxes is not comparable to a salary of the same size; if you are used to thinking in salary terms, add roughly twenty to thirty percent before comparing.

## 4. The script

### Deflecting first, which is what you should try

The person who names a number first anchors the negotiation, and here that person does not have to be you. The recruiter is matching you to requisitions with budgets already attached, so the information genuinely exists on their side.

> "It depends a lot on the role, so it'd help to hear what you're matching me to. What band are the technical requisitions you're placing at the moment?"

Ask this warmly and then **stop talking**. The silence is the tool. Most recruiters will answer, because they do have the number and telling you saves them time.

### If they insist you go first

They often will — "we ask candidates for their expectation." Do not fight it twice. Give the number, attach a reason, and move immediately to a question so the conversation does not stall on the figure.

> "Sure. For a technical role — analyst or backend — I'd be looking at [target] a month, and that's based on bringing both the analysis and the engineering side rather than one of them. Is that in range for what you're placing?"

Two things that does. It ties the number to a justification, which makes it a position rather than a wish. And it hands the next turn back to them, which stops you filling silence by negotiating against yourself.

### If the number they give is below your floor

Do not accept in the room, and do not reject flatly either — the requisition in front of them today is not the only one they will have.

> "That's below where I can go, honestly. For a technical requisition I'd need to be closer to [target]. I'd rather be straight with you now than waste a client interview — but if something comes up in that range I'd be very interested."

That answer keeps you in the pool, keeps the relationship warm, and costs you nothing. Agencies remember candidates who were clear and pleasant about money far longer than they remember candidates who were flexible.

### If they ask what you currently earn

You are not obliged to answer, and in many US jurisdictions employers may not ask — though an offshore placement conversation is not covered by that. Redirect rather than refuse.

> "I'd rather talk about the value for this role than what I've been paid before — my consulting rates have varied a lot by project. For this kind of work I'd be looking at [target]."

## 5. The terms that decide what the number actually means

A monthly figure means nothing until you know the arrangement around it. These are not awkward questions. A company whose entire product is cross-border remote placement will have crisp answers, and hesitation is itself information.

**Who employs me, and who pays me?** Confirm the model out loud: is this a direct contractor relationship with the client, with Remote Leverage as introducer only? Which legal entity appears on the agreement?

**How does payment reach Haiti?** This is the question most likely to produce a surprise, and it is better to have the surprise now. Which currency, which rail — bank wire, Wise, Payoneer, something else — what frequency, and who absorbs the transfer fees. A payment method that does not work from Haiti turns a good offer into no offer, and it is a solvable problem if raised early.

**Is the rate gross, and what am I responsible for?** In a direct-contractor arrangement you handle your own tax. Confirm nothing is withheld and that you are not expecting a net figure that is actually gross.

**What are the hours, and are they fixed?** Is a specific US timezone window contractually required, and is there any weekend or on-call expectation.

**Paid time off, holidays, sick days.** Often none in this model. Better to know than to assume.

**Equipment.** Provided, reimbursed, or your own. If your own, that is a real cost against the rate.

**The guarantee period.** How long, and what happens if the client ends the engagement inside it — for you, not just for the agency.

**Notice, and how the relationship ends.** Both directions.

**Exclusivity or non-circumvention.** Does anything restrict you from working with that client directly later, or with other agencies now. Read this clause; it is the one that quietly matters most.

**Any fee to the candidate — ever.** The answer must be no. A legitimate placement agency is paid by the client. Any request for money from you ends the process, full stop.

---

## 6. Before you sign anything

Read the whole agreement, including the parts that are obviously boilerplate. Do not send government ID numbers, banking details or scanned documents before there is a signed agreement and a named entity to send them to. Do not pay anything. Expect a written contract before you start work rather than after.

The public signal on this company is reasonable — Glassdoor around 4.9 out of 5 across roughly 299 reviews — so none of this is written because something looks wrong. It is written because these are the precautions that apply to every cross-border engagement, and the cost of taking them is a few minutes.

*This is general preparation, not legal or financial advice. For anything binding, a professional who knows Haitian and US contracting is worth the consultation.*
