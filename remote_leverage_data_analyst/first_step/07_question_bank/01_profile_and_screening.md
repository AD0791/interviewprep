# Profile and screening questions

*Q1–Q22. The half of the interview you can fully control, because none of it depends on what they ask — only on how well you have rehearsed what you already know.*

---

## Q1 — Tell me about yourself.

> "I'm a data analyst with about five years of experience, and what's slightly unusual about my background is that I've worked both sides of the data stack. On the analysis side, I've owned reporting for multi-site programmes — building the indicator frameworks, writing the SQL, and shipping the dashboards that leadership and funders actually made decisions from, in Power BI and Looker Studio. On the engineering side, I've built the pipelines underneath: ETL jobs in Apache Beam moving MongoDB data into BigQuery, Python jobs running on AWS, and I've done the unglamorous parts — monitoring runs, chasing failures, reconciling numbers between systems that disagreed. That combination is why this role interested me. You're asking for someone who can write and optimise BigQuery SQL, build dashboards, *and* keep an eye on pipeline health and data quality — and most people do one of those three well. I've also spent a lot of my career working with distributed teams across time zones, in English and French, which I gather is the normal way of working here."

Ninety seconds, four beats: what I am, the analytics proof, the engineering proof, why this role. Learn the beats rather than the words, because memorised text sounds memorised and the first thing an interviewer hears is whether you are reciting.

Two deliberate omissions. It does not say "MEAL specialist" and it does not lead with humanitarian work — not because you are hiding it, but because leading with the sector invites them to file you as sector-specific, and the sector comes up naturally two minutes later when you describe a project. It also does not mention Tableau. Do not raise your own gap in your opening statement; raise it when the subject is Tableau, which is Q91.

## Q2 — Why are you interested in this role?

> "Two reasons, one about the work and one about the business. The work is the shape I want — it's an in-house analytics role where I'd own the numbers end to end rather than being handed a clean table and asked to chart it. Writing the SQL, building the dashboards, and being responsible for the pipelines feeding them is exactly the combination I've been doing, and it's rarer than it sounds. The business is the more interesting part. You charge a one-time flat fee rather than an ongoing margin, which means your revenue tracks placement volume, not headcount under management. That makes fill rate, time to fill and acquisition cost the metrics that actually matter, and it makes candidate supply the growth constraint rather than sales capacity. That's a genuinely interesting measurement problem, and it's not the same problem a traditional staffing firm has."

The second half is what separates this from every other candidate's answer. Most people will say "I'm passionate about data" and describe the role back at them. Deriving the metric priorities from the pricing model shows you understood the business before the call rather than during it.

## Q3 — What do you know about Remote Leverage?

> "You're a Florida-based virtual-assistant placement agency, recruiting English-speaking VAs mainly from Latin America and the Philippines for US businesses. The model that struck me is the one-time flat fee — the client pays once and then pays the VA directly, rather than you taking an ongoing margin, which is your main differentiator against traditional staffing firms, and a partner company handles payroll and compliance. The public signal is strong, around 4.9 on Glassdoor across roughly three hundred reviews. What I couldn't find is the internal picture — how many requisitions you're running, what the recruiting pipeline looks like, which is presumably part of what this role exists to make visible."

Ending on what you could *not* find is a good move: it is honest, it flags that you looked properly, and it hands them an easy opening to describe their own operation, which is information you want.

## Q4 — Walk me through your CV.

Go backwards, not forwards, and spend your time proportionally to relevance. Start with Tekkod today — data and performance work, the N+1 fix, the Looker Studio dashboards. Then Anseye Pou Ayiti, where you defined the KPI layer. Then HANWASH, where you owned indicator frameworks and the operational database for a multi-site programme reporting to international funders. Then the earlier Tekkod engagement, which is where the Apache Beam pipelines into BigQuery live. Then Caris Foundation, which is the data-quality framework and the 40% reporting automation. Compress CassionSoft and the Ministry of Planning into one sentence each.

The framing sentence to open with: *"I'll go backwards, and I'll flag as I go which parts are most relevant to what you're hiring for."* It signals structure, and it gives you permission to skim the parts that do not serve you.

## Q5 — Your job titles say software engineer and M&E officer. How are you a data analyst?

> "Fair question, and it's a real feature of my CV rather than something I'd argue with. The titles come from the Haitian market, where you're usually hired against a project rather than a job family, so the title describes the contract and not the work. If you look at what I actually did in each of those roles, the analyst work is the constant. At Caris I built the indicator framework, wrote the SQL, and produced the weekly KPI dashboards. At HANWASH I owned the indicator tracking tables and the Power BI reporting for external funders. At Tekkod the title says engineer, but the work included the BigQuery datasets and the Looker Studio dashboards on top of them. I've been doing analyst work continuously for about five years — it's just been labelled six different things."

Say it calmly and once. The temptation is to over-argue, and over-arguing is what makes an interviewer think there is something to argue about.

## Q6 — Several of your roles overlap in time. Can you explain that?

> "Yes — those are consulting engagements running alongside a primary programme role, which is standard in the Haitian market, where organisations contract specialists per project rather than hiring full time. The Caris Foundation role was my main programme engagement, and Tekkod and HANWASH were parallel consulting contracts."

One sentence, plainly, then stop and let them move on. Over-explaining is precisely what turns a normal consulting pattern into something that sounds like a problem. If they push further, add: *"Happy to walk through any specific period in detail if it's useful."* That is confident rather than defensive, and it almost always ends the topic.

## Q7 — Why did you go back to Tekkod?

> "An existing relationship and a project that fitted. I'd worked with them from 2021 to 2024 on the backend and the data pipelines, so I knew the codebase, and when they took on a modernisation project they needed someone who wouldn't spend three months learning the system. It's been good work — the performance side especially, which is where the N+1 optimisation came from."

Honest, short, and it hands you a bridge into your strongest technical anecdote if you want it.

## Q8 — Are you currently employed, and why are you looking to move?

Answer straight about the current engagement, and give a forward-looking reason rather than a complaint. Never criticise a current or former employer; it costs you more than any grievance is worth.

> "I am, at Tekkod. What I'm looking for is a role where analytics is the job rather than a component of it. Most of my analytical work has been either inside an engineering role or inside a programme role, and in both cases the analysis was something I did alongside something else. I want to own the numbers as the primary responsibility — and specifically in a commercial setting, because the feedback loop is faster. In a programme cycle you change something and learn whether it worked in a quarter. In a funnel you change something on Monday and see it by Friday."

The last two sentences are the strongest part. They give a genuine, positive reason for the sector move that also answers the *"why leave the NGO world"* question before it is asked.

## Q9 — Why are you moving from the humanitarian sector to a commercial one?

> "The pull rather than the push: faster feedback loops. Programme cycles are long, and you often learn whether an intervention worked long after you could have changed it. A commercial funnel gives you the same analytical problems with a much tighter loop. And the analysis genuinely is the same — a funnel is a funnel, whether what drops out is candidates in a placement pipeline or households in a distribution programme. Coverage against target is quota attainment, cost per person reached is cost per acquisition, and time from announcement to delivery is time to fill. What changes is the vocabulary and what the number gets used for."

That translation table is the heart of your whole positioning, and it is worth being able to produce those three equivalences fluently. It converts "no commercial experience" from a gap into a translation exercise you have already done.

## Q10 — You've never worked as an analyst in a commercial setting. Why should we take that risk?

This is the objection you will actually get, and module 06 is built around it. The full script:

> "It's a fair question and I'd rather answer it concretely than assert that skills transfer. The analysis is genuinely the same — a funnel is a funnel, and coverage against target is quota attainment. What changes is vocabulary. Two things I'd add. First, the quality bar I'm used to is higher than people expect: programme figures are audited externally by funders, and a wrong number can cost the organisation the grant, which is why I build reconciliation checks before I build dashboards — it's a habit, not a policy. Second, rather than tell you I learn quickly, I'd rather show you. When I read your job description I built myself a practice dataset modelling a placement agency — leads, reps, requisitions, submittals, placements, ad spend — and worked the metrics. Two things came out that I'd want to check against your real data. Six and a half percent of leads were never contacted at all — paid for, then never worked, which comes to roughly twenty-four thousand dollars of ad spend once you weight each one by its channel's cost per lead. And when I compared cost per lead with cost per acquisition, the channel with the cheapest leads turned out to be nearly three times more expensive per client. That's the kind of thing I'd be hunting for in week one."

Note what that answer does not do: it never disparages commercial analysts, and it never says "fast learner". It substitutes evidence for the adjective, which is the only way that claim ever lands.

## Q11 — What's your experience with English? This role is entirely in English.

> "Fluent — I've worked in English throughout my career. Both Tekkod engagements have been remote with English-speaking teams, the HANWASH work was through Upwork with an international client, and the funder reporting I've done has been in English. French is my native language and Haitian Creole as well, so I'm working across all three most days."

Then stop. Do not elaborate on your English in English; the answer to this question is the whole conversation, not the sentence. The real preparation for this one is the twenty minutes a day of speaking practice, not the script.

## Q12 — Where are you based, and what time zone would you work in?

> "Port-au-Prince, Haiti. We're on Eastern time — UTC-5 in winter, and Haiti observes daylight saving on the same dates as the US, so I'm aligned with US Eastern all year round with no seasonal drift. That means full overlap with a US business day, every day, which I gather matters here."

This is a genuine advantage over the Philippines-based half of their own candidate pool, who are twelve hours out, so it is worth stating as a positive rather than answering it as a logistics question. **The year-round alignment is the part to say** — plenty of remote candidates are "mostly aligned" and drift by an hour twice a year, and you do not.

## Q13 — What's your internet and power situation?

Answer this directly and without defensiveness. They place people from Latin America and the Philippines for a living, so they have heard this question answered badly and they know what a good answer looks like: specifics, plus a backup.

> "Fibre connection as the primary, with backup power, and a mobile connection as a secondary. I've been working remotely with US and international teams continuously since 2021, including video-heavy work, so this is a solved problem rather than a hope. I have my own laptop and headset."

That wording matches the availability line on your tailored CV exactly, which is deliberate — if they have read the CV, hearing the same claim in the same terms is corroboration. Give the specifics of your actual setup if they probe, but do not volunteer detail you would then have to qualify.

## Q14 — When could you start?

Give a real date rather than "immediately", unless immediately is true and clean. Two weeks reads as professional; "right now" can read as unattached.

> "I'd want to hand over properly at Tekkod, so realistically two weeks from an offer. If there's a reason to move faster I can look at it."

## Q15 — What are your salary expectations?

The posting says $1,920–$2,080 a month. That is a stated range rather than an invitation, so anchor at the top of it and move the conversation to progression, which is where the real value sits.

> "I saw the range in the posting and it's workable. I'd be aiming at the upper end, given I bring the engineering side as well as the analysis — I can maintain the pipelines, not just consume them. Is there a review cycle, or a path for that to move as the scope grows?"

If they push you to name a number first, name the top of their own range rather than inventing one: *"The two thousand and eighty end of what you published."* You cannot be penalised for asking for what they advertised.

## Q16 — Is this a contractor engagement or employment, and how does payment work?

You should be asking this, not just answering it. Have the list ready and deliver it as curiosity rather than suspicion.

> "A few practical things I'd want to understand: is this a contractor engagement or employment, and through which entity? How does payment work for someone based in Haiti — which currency, which method, what frequency? Are the hours fixed to a US time zone or is there flexibility? Is there paid time off, and is equipment provided or expected?"

A company whose entire product is cross-border remote work will have crisp answers to all of that. An inability to answer clearly is itself information, and worth noticing.

## Q17 — What are you looking for in your next role?

> "Three things. Ownership of the numbers rather than a piece of them — I want to be the person who's accountable when a figure is wrong, because that's the only way you get to build the checks that prevent it. Proximity to decisions, so the analysis actually changes something rather than being filed. And a business small enough that I can hold the whole model in my head, which is much harder in a large organisation and much more satisfying when it works."

## Q18 — Where do you see yourself in five years?

Avoid both the fantasy answer and the empty one. Anchor it to a direction that the role plausibly serves.

> "Deepening rather than switching. I'd like to be the person who owns the analytics layer for a business end to end — the metric definitions, the models underneath them, and the reporting on top — and increasingly to be the one shaping which questions get asked rather than only answering them. Whether that's called senior analyst or analytics lead matters less to me than the scope."

## Q19 — What's your greatest strength?

> "Distrust of my own numbers, honestly. Before I publish anything I reconcile it against a second source, and I've built that into how I work rather than doing it when I feel uncertain — because the times you feel certain are exactly when you don't check. It comes from the funder-audit environment I trained in, where a wrong figure has real consequences. It makes me slightly slower on the first delivery and considerably faster afterwards, because nothing comes back."

## Q20 — What's your greatest weakness?

Use the real one. A disguised strength is transparent and it costs you credibility for the rest of the conversation.

> "I have a tendency to build the durable version before the urgent one. Someone asks for a number and I start designing the pipeline that would produce it reliably every week, when what they needed was the number by Thursday. What I do about it now is deliberately separate the two: deliver what's needed this week first, in whatever rough form works, and then go back and harden it if it's going to recur. Naming that split out loud — 'here's the quick answer, and here's what it would take to make it repeatable' — has been the thing that fixed it."

## Q21 — How do you handle critical feedback?

> "Reproduce before responding. If someone tells me a number is wrong, my first move is to go and check rather than explain — partly because they're sometimes right, and partly because arguing before verifying is how you end up defending something indefensible. If it turns out I was wrong, I say so quickly and plainly, and then I add the check that would have caught it, because a correction without a process change is just an apology."

## Q22 — Why should we hire you?

Close with a summary rather than new material. Three sentences, then stop.

> "Because you're asking for three things that don't usually come together: someone who can write and optimise the SQL, build the dashboards, and keep the pipelines underneath them healthy. I've done all three, and the pipeline half is the part most analyst candidates can't do. And because the first thing I'd do here is reconciliation rather than decoration — I'd rather find your data problems in week one than have you find them in my chart in month three."

## Where you might slip

Do not apologise for the Tableau gap in an answer that was not about Tableau. Do not describe yourself as a software engineer who also does data — invert it every time. Do not over-explain the overlapping dates. Do not use "MEAL" as a self-description without immediately translating it, because to an American commercial audience it is an opaque acronym. And do not fill silence after a good answer; the two seconds after you finish are the interviewer's turn, and taking them back is how a strong answer becomes a rambling one.
